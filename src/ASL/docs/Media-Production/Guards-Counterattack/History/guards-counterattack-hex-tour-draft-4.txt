THE GUARDS COUNTERATTACK: ACROSS THE ROAD

1. Scene 1: Two buildings
PRESENTER

[calm, clear voice] The Guards Counterattack. Across the Road.

Stalingrad, the sixth of October, nineteen forty-two.

In the north of the city, German and Soviet troops are fighting through the industrial district. The factories, workers' housing, and streets between them form a connected battlefield. Holding a workshop or an apartment building can protect an approach to the next position. Losing one can expose the men still holding nearby.

The Soviet formations fighting in this district include the Thirty-seventh Guards Rifle Division and the Three Hundred and Eighth Rifle Division. The Guards have been formed from airborne troops and are now fighting as infantry. The Three Hundred and Eighth, raised in Siberia, has entered the city fighting under Vasily Chuikov's Sixty-second Army.

Our scenario draws on this struggle around the approaches to the Dzerzhinsky Tractor Works. Its premise is a local Soviet counterattack against German infantry who have gained ground and must now hold it. The Guards are to drive them from their buildings, with other Soviet riflemen already established nearby.

An attack on this scale has an immediate purpose. Recovering a position may help neighboring troops hold on, interfere with the enemy's next advance, or restore room to move. But taking a building is only part of the task. The attackers must arrive with enough men still organized to defend it.

The attack has not begun. On either side of the road, men prepare without knowing what their opponents intend.

PRESENTER

The fighting centers on stone buildings separated by paved roads and open ground, with smaller wooden buildings and patches of woods elsewhere on the board. A building can shelter several squads while dividing their views. Men on one face may see an enemy position that is hidden from men only a hex away.

We will use hex coordinates to locate each stop. F three names both a particular hex and the larger building containing it. The Guards occupy four hexes of that building. The German building called F five likewise extends beyond F five itself. Every unit shown here starts on the ground floor.

The Soviet side has twenty-one squads: twelve Guards squads concentrated in one building group, and nine ordinary rifle squads distributed among four others. Two leaders and a commissar accompany them. Their only separate support weapon is a medium machine gun.

The German side has thirteen rifle squads and five leaders, spread among five building groups. Six light machine guns, one medium machine gun, and one heavy machine gun support them. There are no tanks, artillery batteries, or later reinforcements in this order of battle.

The Soviets move first and have five turns. At the end, they must control at least two more German starting stone buildings than the Germans control Soviet starting stone buildings. Taking two while losing one is not enough. The five German buildings are F five, K five, I seven, M seven, and M nine. The four Soviet stone buildings are F three, J two, M two, and N four. The wooden building at N two does not count toward this building objective.

The Soviets can also win by finishing with at least three times as many unbroken squad-equivalents as the Germans. Infantry that can still act matters as much as the ground gained.

The Germans must prevent both results. That makes the Soviet starting buildings important too. The Germans can threaten those positions as well as defend their own.
